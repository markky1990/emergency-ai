## 1. Armed attack or gunman: Escape, Hide, Tell

- Guide id: `armed-attack`  Fingerprint: `493a7bff`
- Category: Attacks and threats  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Australia-New Zealand Counter-Terrorism Committee

1. ESCAPE: If there is a safe way out, move quickly and quietly away from danger. Leave your belongings. Help others if you can, but do not stop.
2. HIDE: If you cannot escape, stay out of sight. Put a sturdy barrier between you and the attacker, such as a locked or barricaded door and solid cover. Silence your phone, including vibration.
3. TELL: When it is safe, call 000. Give the location, the number of attackers, descriptions, weapons, and where you are.
4. Keep reviewing your situation and your options.
5. Stay where you are hiding until police tell you it is safe. Armed police may move past you to find the attacker.
6. When police reach you: stay calm, keep your hands up and visible, and follow instructions. Do not run toward them.

## 2. Bomb threat or suspicious item

- Guide id: `bomb-threat`  Fingerprint: `a98851cd`
- Category: Attacks and threats  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Australian Government National Security: IED Guidelines for Crowded Places

1. Do not touch, tilt, or tamper with a suspicious item. Clear the area around it.
2. Do not use mobile phones or radios within about 25 metres of it. Move further for a vehicle: at least 100 metres.
3. Call 000 from a safe distance. Say where it is and what you saw. Note its position and what it looks like.
4. If a threat comes by phone: stay calm, keep the caller talking, note the number if it shows, and record the call if you can. Write down the exact words, and ask where it is and when it will go off. Tell building security and call 000.
5. If a threat comes by email or social media: do not reply, forward, or delete it. Note the sender or username and call 000.
6. After an explosion: move away. Another may follow. Help injured people if it is safe. Do not go back.

- Guide id: `emergency-numbers-au`  Fingerprint: `54d9b9c7`
- Category: Basics  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Triple Zero Australia / Healthdirect / state emergency services

## 4. Bushfire

- Guide id: `bushfire`  Fingerprint: `09c8e40f`
- Category: Disasters  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Country Fire Authority (Vic) / Country Fire Service (SA) / NSW Rural Fire Service

1. Leaving early is the safest option. Do not wait to see flames. Leave when you get a warning, or before on bad fire danger days.
2. Listen to local ABC radio, and check your state fire service app or website.
3. Wear long cotton or wool clothes, sturdy boots, gloves, a hat, and a damp cloth or a P2 mask (a tight-fitting mask that filters smoke) over your nose and mouth.
4. If you are caught on the road: turn back and drive away from the fire if it is safe. If you cannot, shelter in a solid building if there is one very close. Only if there is nowhere else, stay in your car.
5. In a car: park clear of bush, trees, and other cars, ideally behind a barrier such as a brick wall, facing the fire. Shut all windows and vents, turn off the air conditioning and the engine, and get down below window level under a dry pure wool blanket. Do not wet the blanket, because it can cause steam burns.
6. After the fire front passes, hold your hand up to feel the air before you get out of the car.
7. If you are trapped at home: stay inside a solid building. Move to a room on the side away from the fire, with two exits if possible. Watch for embers and put out small spot fires.
8. Call 000 if life is in danger.

**Warning shown:** The heat given off by a fire can kill, even from a distance. Do not shelter in the open. Staying low, covered up, and shielded from the fire is critical. Fire services differ on the engine: Victoria's CFA says turn it off as the fire front arrives, while Queensland says leave it running with headlights and hazard lights on. Follow your own state fire service.

## 5. Chemical spill, toxic smoke, or gas cloud

- Guide id: `chemical-shelter`  Fingerprint: `55dd6d8b`
- Category: Disasters  (urgent guide)
- Source named: CDC / FEMA Ready.gov

1. Move away from the spill, smoke, or gas. Do not walk through it. Walk into the wind if you can, so it blows the gas away from you.
2. Go inside the nearest building. Close and lock all windows and doors. Turn off air conditioners, fans, and heaters that draw in outside air.
3. Go to an inner room with few or no windows, as high in the building as you can, and ideally with water. Seal gaps with plastic sheeting and tape or wet towels.
4. Call 000. Listen to local ABC radio for instructions.
5. If a chemical got on your skin or clothing: take off outer clothes (cut them off rather than pulling over your head), put them in a bag, and wash the skin with lots of soap and water.
6. A sealed room is meant for a few hours only. Stay until authorities say it is clear, then air out the building.

## 6. Earthquake

- Guide id: `earthquake`  Fingerprint: `9d4f576b`
- Category: Disasters  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Geoscience Australia / Victoria SES

1. Drop to your hands and knees. Cover your head and neck, ideally under a sturdy table or desk. Hold on until the shaking stops.
2. Stay where you are until the shaking stops. Do not run outside.
3. Stay away from windows, glass, mirrors, and heavy things that can fall.
4. If you are outside, move away from buildings, power lines, and trees.
5. After the shaking: check yourself and others for injury. Expect aftershocks.
6. If you smell gas, hear hissing, or see damage, turn off the gas if safe, and leave.
7. If you are near the coast and the shaking was strong or long, or the sea pulls back, move to high ground at once. See the tsunami guide.

## 7. Flood

- Guide id: `flood`  Fingerprint: `61469b87`
- Category: Disasters  (urgent guide)
- Source named: State Emergency Service (NSW and Victoria)

1. Move to higher ground early. Do not wait for water to reach you.
2. Never walk, swim, ride, or drive through floodwater. It can be deeper than it looks and can hide damaged roads and bridges, debris, and other dangers. If it is flooded, forget it.
3. Floodwater is dirty. It carries sewage, chemicals, and sharp debris. Keep children and pets out.
4. If it is safe, switch off power, gas, and water at the mains.
5. Call 132 500 for State Emergency Service help in a flood or storm. Call 000 if life is in danger.
6. If trapped, go to the highest safe place in the building and signal for help.
7. After a flood, do not use electrical items that got wet. Throw away food that touched floodwater.

## 8. Nuclear or radiation emergency

- Guide id: `radiation`  Fingerprint: `237a6c88`
- Category: Disasters  (urgent guide)
- Source named: CDC radiation emergency guidance

1. Get inside the nearest solid building straight away. Go to a basement or the middle of the building, away from outside walls and the roof.
2. Stay inside. Close windows and doors. Turn off fans, air conditioners, and heaters that bring in outside air.
3. Stay tuned. Use a battery radio for official instructions. Stay inside at least 24 hours unless told otherwise.
4. If you were outside: take off your outer clothes and seal them in a bag. This can remove most radioactive material. Wash your skin and hair with soap and water.
5. Do not take potassium iodide tablets unless authorities tell you to.
6. Eat and drink only sealed food and water until told that it is safe.

**Warning shown:** Shelter and stay informed is the main protection. Distance and time reduce exposure.

## 9. Tsunami

- Guide id: `tsunami`  Fingerprint: `df80b24b`
- Category: Disasters  (urgent guide)
- Source named: NSW State Emergency Service

1. Natural warning signs: a strong or long earthquake near the coast, the sea suddenly pulling back, or a loud roar from the ocean. Move to high ground or inland at once. You may not get an official warning.
2. Go on foot if you can. Roads may jam. Go as high and as far inland as you can quickly reach. Follow local signs and maps that show where to go.
3. Never go to the beach to watch.
4. A tsunami is a series of waves. The first may not be the biggest. They can keep coming for hours.
5. Stay away from the coast until the official all-clear.
6. Call the SES on 132 500 for tsunami help, and 000 if life is in danger.

## 10. Adult choking

- Guide id: `choking-adult`  Fingerprint: `d3bfcc0e`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Healthdirect / St John Ambulance Australia / Australian Red Cross

1. Ask: are you choking? If they can still cough or speak, encourage them to keep coughing.
2. If they cannot speak, cough, or breathe: call 000, or ask someone else to.
3. Lean them forward. Give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Check after each blow to see if the object has come out.
4. If that does not work, give up to 5 chest pushes. Place your hand on the lower half of the breastbone and push sharply. Make them slower and harder than CPR pushes.
5. Keep alternating back blows and chest pushes until the object comes out or help arrives.
6. If they stop responding, lower them to the ground and start CPR.

## 11. Adult not breathing: CPR (chest pushes and breaths)

- Guide id: `cpr-adult`  Fingerprint: `3d009ba0`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Australian Resuscitation Council / St John Ambulance Australia

1. Shout for help. Call 000 if you can, or ask someone else to. Put the phone on speaker.
2. Lay the person on their back on a firm surface.
3. Put the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone, in the middle of the chest. Put your other hand on top.
4. Push hard and fast: at least 5 centimetres deep (about one third of the chest), 100 to 120 pushes a minute. Let the chest come back up fully each time.
5. If you are trained and willing, give 2 breaths after every 30 pushes. If not, keep pushing without stopping. Any CPR is better than none.
6. If an AED (a machine that can restart the heart) is available, switch it on and follow its voice instructions.
7. Do not stop until help arrives, the person starts to move or breathe normally, or you cannot continue.

**Warning shown:** Do this only if the person is not responding and not breathing normally. Gasping is not normal breathing.

## 12. Drowning

- Guide id: `drowning`  Fingerprint: `507ec9a7`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Australian Resuscitation Council (ANZCOR 9.3.2) / Royal Life Saving Society Australia

1. Do not put yourself at risk. Reach with a pole, rope, or throw something that floats. Go in only if you are trained and it is safe.
2. Call 000.
3. Get them out of the water and lay them flat.
4. Check breathing. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR with chest pushes and breaths (30 pushes then 2 breaths). Breaths matter in drowning. Trained rescuers may give 2 breaths first.
5. Do not try to drain water from the lungs.
6. If they are breathing, roll them onto their side and keep them warm. All near-drowning cases must go to hospital.

## 13. Electric shock

- Guide id: `electric-shock`  Fingerprint: `768d8568`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Ausgrid / Healthdirect / St John Ambulance Australia

1. Do not touch the person until the power is off. Electricity can pass through them to you.
2. Switch off the power at the switch or main. If you cannot, use a dry non-metal object, such as a wooden broom handle, to push them clear.
3. Fallen power lines: stay at least 8 metres (about 2 car lengths) away. Call 000. Do not go near, even to help. Warn others away.
4. If the person is touching a high-voltage line, do not approach. Wait for the power company or emergency crews to cut the power.
5. Call 000 for any serious electric shock, or if they passed out or have abnormal breathing or heartbeat.
6. If they are not responding and not breathing normally, start CPR.
7. Cool burns under running water for 20 minutes and cover loosely. Even after a mild shock, get a medical check, because it can affect the heart.

## 14. Poisoning, chemicals, or overdose

- Guide id: `poisoning`  Fingerprint: `1cec6cf1`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Healthdirect / Poisons Information Centre

1. If the person is unconscious, having a seizure, or having trouble breathing, call 000.
2. Otherwise call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 (24 hours). Tell them what, how much, and when.
3. Do not make the person vomit. Do not give food or drink unless told to.
4. If a chemical is on skin: take off clothing that has the chemical on it and rinse the skin under running water for at least 20 minutes.
5. If a chemical is in the eye: hold the eyelids open and rinse gently under running water for 20 minutes. Do not rub.
6. Keep the container or label to show the ambulance.

## 15. Severe bleeding

- Guide id: `bleeding-severe`  Fingerprint: `90aef3d6`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: St John Ambulance Australia / Australian Resuscitation Council

1. Call 000. Press firmly and continuously on the wound with your hands, using a clean cloth or clothing if you have it.
2. Do not lift the cloth to check. If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top and keep pressing.
3. If you have a dressing or bandage, put a firm pad over the wound and bandage it tightly. Keep pressing on top.
4. A tourniquet (a tight band that stops the blood flow) is only for bleeding on an arm or leg that is life threatening and that firm pressure has not stopped. A purpose-made tourniquet is best.
5. Place it 5 to 8 centimetres (about 3 fingers wide) above the bleeding, directly on the skin, never over a joint or the wound. Tighten until the bleeding stops.
6. Write down the time you applied it. Do not loosen or remove it. Keep the person still and warm until the ambulance arrives.

## 16. Snake bite (Australia)

- Guide id: `snake-bite`  Fingerprint: `15c6b0dc`
- Category: First aid  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Healthdirect / St John Ambulance Australia / Better Health Channel

1. Call 000 right away. Keep the person calm and completely still. Do not let them walk.
2. Do not wash the bite site. Venom on the skin helps identify the snake.
3. Put a firm, wide bandage (10 to 15 centimetres) over the bite site straight away.
4. Then wrap another bandage over the whole limb, starting just above the fingers or toes and going up as far as you can. It should be tight, but should not cause numbness, tingling, or a colour change in the fingers or toes.
5. Splint the limb to stop it moving. Mark the bite site on the bandage.
6. Do not cut the bite, suck it, or tie a tight band around the limb. Do not remove the bandage.
7. Do not try to catch the snake.

## 17. Carbon monoxide (invisible poison gas): generators, stoves, and barbecues

- Guide id: `co-poisoning`  Fingerprint: `5b261dc7`
- Category: Home safety  (urgent guide)
- Source named: CDC (US guidance). Check your state energy safety authority for local rules.

1. Never run a generator, barbecue, camping stove, or charcoal grill inside a house, garage, tent, or near windows.
2. Signs: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, feeling sleepy.
3. If you suspect it: get everyone into fresh air immediately.
4. Do not go back inside until it is safe. Call emergency services if you can.
5. Run generators outside, at least 6 metres (20 feet) from windows, doors, and vents, with the exhaust (where the fumes come out) pointing away. Use a battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm.

## 18. Diarrhoea, vomiting, and dehydration (losing too much water)

- Guide id: `dehydration-diarrhoea`  Fingerprint: `9a3f31a0`
- Category: Illness that spreads  (urgent guide)
- Source named: WHO / Healthdirect

1. The main danger is losing too much water. Give small, frequent sips of fluid, such as rehydration drink (oral rehydration solution) from a pharmacy. Sachets are best.
2. If you have no sachets, WHO gives a home mix: 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in 1 litre of safe water (boiled and cooled). Measure carefully. Too much sugar or salt is harmful.
3. Give a sip every few minutes. If the person vomits, wait 10 minutes and start again slowly.
4. Keep eating normally if they can.
5. Get medical help for blood in the poo, a high fever, little or no urine, a very dry mouth, dizziness, drowsiness, or confusion, or if the diarrhoea keeps going.
6. A baby under 6 months with diarrhoea needs to see a doctor right away. Small children, older people, and pregnant women need help sooner.
7. Wash hands carefully. Keep sick people away from food preparation.

## 19. Asthma attack

- Guide id: `asthma`  Fingerprint: `13e433c6`
- Category: Medical emergency  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Asthma Australia

1. Sit the person upright. Stay calm. Stay with them.
2. Give 4 puffs of a blue or grey reliever puffer, one puff at a time, using a spacer if you have one (a tube that fits on the puffer). Take 4 breaths after each puff.
3. Wait 4 minutes. If they still cannot breathe normally, give 4 more puffs.
4. If there is still no improvement, call 000. Keep giving 4 puffs every 4 minutes until help arrives.
5. Call 000 straight away if they are not breathing, suddenly get worse, or are turning blue.
6. If they have a known allergy and sudden breathing trouble, give their adrenaline pen first.

## 20. Baby or small child choking

- Guide id: `choking-baby`  Fingerprint: `22e62d03`
- Category: Medical emergency  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Australian Resuscitation Council / Red Cross

1. If the baby cannot cry, cough, or breathe, call 000.
2. Lay the baby face down along your forearm, with the head lower than the body. Support the head and jaw.
3. Give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
4. If that fails, turn the baby onto their back, head low. Give up to 5 chest pushes with two fingers on the lower half of the breastbone.
5. Repeat 5 back blows and 5 chest pushes until the object comes out or the baby stops responding.
6. If the baby stops responding, start CPR.

## 21. CPR for a child or baby (chest pushes and breaths)

- Guide id: `cpr-child-baby`  Fingerprint: `64495a96`
- Category: Medical emergency  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Australian Resuscitation Council / St John Ambulance Australia / Healthdirect

1. Shout for help. Call 000 on speaker so you can keep both hands free.
2. Lay them on a firm flat surface.
3. Child (1 year and over): push on the lower half of the breastbone with the heel of one hand (or two hands for a bigger child), about one third of the chest depth, around 5 centimetres.
4. Baby (under 1 year): push with two fingers on the lower half of the breastbone, about one third of the chest depth, around 4 centimetres.
5. Push 100 to 120 times a minute. After every 30 pushes, give 2 gentle breaths if you can. For a baby, cover mouth and nose with your mouth.
6. Use an AED if you have one, with child pads if available.
7. Do not stop until help arrives.

## 22. Emergency childbirth

- Guide id: `childbirth-emergency`  Fingerprint: `9efabc93`
- Category: Medical emergency  (urgent guide)
- Source named: St John Ambulance NZ / Western Sydney University / The Conversation

1. Call 000 and stay on the line. The operator can talk you through it. Stay calm.
2. Help the mother get low and comfortable, for example half sitting or on her side. Put towels or sheets under her so the baby has a soft landing.
3. Wash your hands if you can. Do not pull the baby. Let it come out by itself and catch it gently with a clean towel.
4. Once born, dry the baby and keep it warm. Put the baby on the mother's chest, skin to skin, and cover both with a warm towel or blanket.
5. Do not cut or tie the cord. Do not pull on it. The placenta can wait until the ambulance arrives.
6. If the baby is not breathing, follow the operator's instructions and start baby CPR (see the baby CPR guide).

## 23. Heart attack

- Guide id: `heart-attack`  Fingerprint: `f91b8990`
- Category: Medical emergency  (urgent guide)
- Source named: Healthdirect / St John Ambulance Australia / Heart Foundation

1. Signs: pain, pressure or tightness in the chest. It may spread to the arm, neck, jaw, or back. Sweating, nausea, breathlessness, feeling faint.
2. Call 000 right away. Do not drive yourself.

## 54. Leaving home in an emergency

- Guide id: `evacuate-home`  Fingerprint: `560617c9`
- Category: Basics
- Source named: NSW SES / Brisbane City Council / Fraser Coast Regional Council / Queensland Government

2. Take your emergency kit, medicines, phone and charger, and important papers such as ID and insurance.

7. Pets are usually not allowed in emergency shelters, except registered assistance animals. Plan for your pets early, and pack food, water, and a lead.

## 55. Survival priorities: the rule of threes

- Guide id: `rule-of-threes`  Fingerprint: `cd067546`
- Category: Basics
- Source named: US Air Force Arctic Survival School fact sheet (rule of thumb)

1. This is a rough rule of thumb, not an exact rule. Children, older people, and sick people have less time.
2. About 3 minutes without air. Fix breathing and heavy bleeding first.

6. Stay calm, stay put if it is safe, and avoid risks. An injury now is much harder to fix later.

## 56. Caring for a sick person at home

## 57. Dirty floodwater, food, and mud

- Guide id: `floodwater-contamination`  Fingerprint: `1ff5e806`

- Source named: Better Health Channel / Queensland Health

## 58. Disinfecting surfaces with bleach

- Guide id: `disinfect-surfaces`  Fingerprint: `db1a137d`
- Category: Illness that spreads
- Source named: CDC

1. Use plain household bleach with 5 to 6 percent chlorine. Do not use scented or splashless bleach, or bleach with added cleaners. Wear gloves and work in a well-place with fresh air.
2. Mix 20 millilitres (4 teaspoons) of bleach into 1 litre of cold water. Make a fresh batch each day.
3. Clean the surface with soap and water first to remove dirt.
4. Apply the bleach solution and keep the surface wet for at least 1 minute. Follow the product label if it gives a different time. Rinse if the surface touches food or skin.
5. Never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar, or other cleaners. It makes poisonous gas.
6. Keep bleach away from children and from food.

## 62. Finding food in the wild: be careful

- Guide id: `wild-food`  Fingerprint: `d9e16df7`
- Category: Shelter and food
- Source named: NSW Health / Better Health Channel / Healthdirect

1. Do not eat any wild mushroom, plant, or berry unless you are 100 percent sure what it is. There is no home test, and cooking, soaking, or peeling does not remove the poison.
2. You can survive weeks without food but only days without water. Do not take risks for food.
3. Fish, shellfish, and meat must be cooked well. Do not eat shellfish from water that may be polluted.

7. If you or someone else ate a wild mushroom or plant and you are unsure, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 straight away. You may not feel sick for 30 minutes to a day. Call 000 if the person is unwell.

## 63. Food safety in a power cut

## 64. Making a fire safely

- Guide id: `fire-safe`  Fingerprint: `146fb94c`
- Category: Shelter and food
- Source named: Country Fire Authority / Queensland Fire Department / NSW Rural Fire Service

1. Check fire bans first. In Australia, lighting a fire on a Total Fire Ban day is illegal.
2. Clear a 3 metre circle down to bare soil. Keep it away from trees, tents, and dry grass. Keep water or sand close.
3. Collect three sizes: tinder (dry grass, bark shavings), kindling (pencil-thin sticks), and fuel (wrist-thick wood).
4. Make a small pile of tinder with kindling leaned over it. Light the tinder from the windward side. Add bigger sticks slowly.
5. Never leave a fire alone. Do not use petrol or other fuel to light it.
6. To put it out: pour on plenty of water (not sand or dirt, because the fire can restart), stir the ashes, and pour on more water. It must be cold to the touch.

## 65. Making a shelter

- Guide id: `shelter-build`  Fingerprint: `0e3cc6fa`
- Category: Shelter and food
- Source named: Victoria Police / NSW National Parks / Better Health Channel

## 69. Boil water to make it safe to drink

- Guide id: `water-boil`  Fingerprint: `340e92c5`
- Category: Water
- Source named: CDC (boil-water guidance)

1. If the water is cloudy, let it settle, then pour it through a clean cloth or coffee filter.
2. Bring the water to a full, rolling boil. Big bubbles, not just small ones at the edge.

## 71. Treat water with household bleach

- Guide id: `water-bleach`  Fingerprint: `4711fb4b`
- Category: Water
- Source named: CDC (emergency water disinfection)

1. Use plain household bleach with 5 to 9 percent chlorine. No scent, no added cleaners.
2. Filter cloudy water through a clean cloth first.
3. Clear water: add 2 drops of bleach per litre (8 drops per 4 litres or 1 gallon).
4. Cloudy water: double it. 4 drops per litre.
5. Stir, then wait 30 minutes before drinking.
6. The water should smell slightly of chlorine. If it does not, treat it again or boil it instead.

**Warning shown:** Bleach does not work well against parasites such as Cryptosporidium. Boiling is best. Do not use old bleach, scented bleach, or bleach with added cleaners. Water purification tablets also work. Follow the packet.

## 72. Dangerously cold: hypothermia (body gets too cold)

- Guide id: `hypothermia`  Fingerprint: `ee132591`
- Category: Weather
- Source named: Better Health Channel / Healthdirect

1. Signs: shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, confusion, drowsiness.
2. Get the person out of the wind and rain. Handle them gently.
3. Take off wet clothes. Replace with dry ones. Cover their head, and wrap in blankets, coats, or sleeping bags.
4. Insulate them from the cold ground with a mat, branches, or bags.
5. Warm them slowly with blankets and warm clothing. Give warm drinks if they are awake and can swallow. No alcohol.
6. Do not use direct heat or a hot bath. Do not rub their arms or legs.
7. If they are confused, drowsy, or unconscious, call 000. Start CPR if they are not breathing normally.

**Warning shown:** Rough handling can cause cardiac arrest in severe hypothermia. Move the person gently.
